# Linguistic Markers of Personality

The evidence base for all framework profiling. These marker families draw on psycholinguistic research (notably Pennebaker's function-word studies and LIWC-style category analysis, and lexical studies of Big Five language correlates). Individual markers are weak signals; convergent patterns across families are the real evidence.

## How to use this reference

- Extract markers mechanically first (count, quote, locate) — interpretation comes after.
- Function words (pronouns, articles, prepositions) are produced with little conscious control, so they resist impression management better than content words. Weight them accordingly in edited or performative genres.
- Always compare against genre baseline: low first-person usage in a technical spec means nothing; in a personal essay it means something.

## Marker families

### 1. Pronoun patterns

| Marker | Associated tendencies |
|---|---|
| High first-person singular (I, me, my) | Self-focus; in emotional contexts, correlates with distress/neuroticism; also plain informality |
| First-person plural (we, our) | Group orientation, agreeableness; in leaders, inclusive framing |
| Second person (you) | Audience orientation, extraversion in conversational registers; instructional stance in others |
| Third-person others (he, she, they, names) | Social attentiveness, interest in people (extraversion, agreeableness) |
| Low pronoun / high article-noun density | Formal, categorical thinking style; correlates with analytic orientation |

### 2. Emotion vocabulary

| Marker | Associated tendencies |
|---|---|
| Positive emotion words (love, great, enjoy) | Extraversion, agreeableness, low neuroticism |
| Negative emotion words (worry, hate, awful) | Neuroticism when pervasive across topics (trait), situational when topic-bound (state) |
| Anxiety words vs. anger words vs. sadness words | Differentiate the flavor of negative affect — anxious vigilance vs. confrontational vs. withdrawn |
| Emotional range and granularity | Rich, precise emotion vocabulary suggests openness and emotional awareness |

### 3. Cognitive-process words

| Marker | Associated tendencies |
|---|---|
| Insight words (realize, understand, means) | Reflectiveness, openness |
| Causal words (because, therefore, hence) | Analytic thinking, need for coherence |
| Comparative/conditional constructions (if, unless, whereas) | Complexity tolerance, deliberation |
| Absolutist terms (always, never, completely) | Low complexity tolerance; when pervasive, rigidity or emotional intensity |

### 4. Certainty and hedging

| Marker | Associated tendencies |
|---|---|
| Hedges (perhaps, might, I think, sort of) | Intellectual humility or anxiety/low assertiveness — disambiguate with other families |
| Certainty markers (clearly, obviously, definitely) | Confidence or dominance; stacked, they suggest a persuasive/assertive stance |
| Qualifier density overall | High = deliberative, cautious (conscientiousness or neuroticism); low = decisive or impulsive |

### 5. Social orientation

| Marker | Associated tendencies |
|---|---|
| Social words (friend, talk, team, party) | Extraversion |
| Politeness routines, softeners, gratitude | Agreeableness |
| Direct requests, imperatives, unsoftened disagreement | Dominance, low agreeableness (or high status/urgency — check context) |
| Perspective-taking phrases ("from their view," "understandably") | Empathy, agreeableness |

### 6. Structure, order, and time

| Marker | Associated tendencies |
|---|---|
| Lists, numbering, systematic organization | Conscientiousness, J-preference |
| Time/scheduling words (deadline, plan, before, schedule) | Conscientiousness, future orientation |
| Achievement words (goal, win, earn, improve) | Conscientiousness (industriousness facet), D-style drive |
| Loose associative structure, digressions, parentheticals | Openness and spontaneity, or low conscientiousness — judge by whether digressions are productive |
| Past/present/future tense balance | Retrospective vs. immediate vs. anticipatory orientation |

### 7. Energy and pace

| Marker | Associated tendencies |
|---|---|
| Exclamations, intensifiers (so, really, totally), emphatic caps | Extraversion, enthusiasm (I-style) |
| Short punchy sentences, rapid topic shifts | High energy, action orientation |
| Long measured sentences, even pacing | Deliberation, steadiness |
| Humor, playfulness, wordplay | Extraversion + openness |

### 8. Abstraction and imagination

| Marker | Associated tendencies |
|---|---|
| Abstract concepts, theory-talk, "what if" framing | Openness, N-preference |
| Concrete sensory detail, specifics, step-by-step accounts | S-preference, practical orientation (not a deficit — a style) |
| Novel metaphors, cross-domain analogies | Openness (imagination facet) |
| Unusual word choices, rare vocabulary | Openness; verbal ability |

### 9. Values and motivation language

| Marker | Associated tendencies |
|---|---|
| Correctness/duty words (should, must, proper, right) | Conscientiousness; Enneagram 1-ish framing |
| Helping/relational framing (for them, support, take care) | Agreeableness; Enneagram 2-ish framing |
| Image/achievement framing (impressive, success, recognized) | Enneagram 3-ish framing; D/I styles |
| Uniqueness/authenticity framing (real, genuine, unlike others) | Enneagram 4-ish framing; openness |
| Knowledge/competence framing (understand, figure out, expertise) | Enneagram 5-ish framing |
| Security/risk framing (safe, risky, what could go wrong) | Enneagram 6-ish framing; neuroticism (anxiety facet) |
| Possibility/experience framing (exciting, options, adventure) | Enneagram 7-ish framing; extraversion + openness |
| Control/strength framing (handle it, in charge, weak/strong) | Enneagram 8-ish framing; dominance |
| Harmony/comfort framing (fine either way, no big deal, settle) | Enneagram 9-ish framing; agreeableness |

## Anti-patterns in marker interpretation

- **Topic contamination**: a text about deadlines will contain time words regardless of the author's conscientiousness. Normalize against what the topic forces.
- **Single-marker leaps**: one "obviously" does not make a dominant personality. Require repetition and cross-family convergence.
- **Reverse scoring neglect**: absence of a marker is weak evidence of the opposite trait — formal genres suppress most markers.
- **Length illusion**: longer samples surface more of everything; compare rates (per 100 words), never raw counts, when contrasting texts.
