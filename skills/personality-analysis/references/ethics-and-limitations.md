# Ethics and Limitations

Load and apply this file before every personality analysis. These rules are not boilerplate; they change what the analysis says and whether it happens at all.

## Hard rules

1. **No clinical claims.** Never diagnose, suggest, or rule out mental-health conditions, disorders, or clinical constructs (depression, narcissism-the-disorder, autism, etc.) from writing. If asked, decline that part and explain that text analysis cannot do this; suggest a qualified professional for genuine concerns. This does not bar discussing a condition the requester self-discloses (e.g., their own ADHD): it is fine to talk about how it may relate to their writing and habits, as long as the analysis neither confirms nor questions the diagnosis itself.
2. **No consequential-decision profiles.** Decline analyses intended to drive hiring, firing, admissions, lending, insurance, legal, or custody decisions. Text-based personality inference does not meet the validity bar for decisions about people's lives, and in employment contexts may be unlawful.
3. **No manipulation kits.** Decline requests framed as "how do I exploit/pressure/deceive this person given their type." Communication-style guidance for cooperation is fine; playbooks for manipulation are not.
4. **Third parties get reduced scope.** When the author hasn't consented (analyzing a colleague's emails, an ex's messages, a public figure's posts):
   - Prefer reframing to *communication-style* analysis (observable behavior) over personality claims.
   - Never analyze private correspondence of a third party for adversarial purposes.
   - Public figures' public writing may be discussed as commentary, with extra hedging and no clinical language.
5. **Minors**: keep analyses developmental and constructive; personality is unstable in adolescence and every claim gets an extra confidence discount.

## Validity limitations — must shape the report

- **Modest correlations.** Even the best text-based trait inference achieves modest correlations with validated questionnaires. Every profile is a set of hypotheses, not measurements.
- **Sample-size floor.** Under ~300 words, or a single genre, most claims should be "low confidence" and the report must say why. Refuse to produce a confident-sounding profile from a tweet.
- **Genre masking.** Formal, edited, or templated writing (cover letters, legal writing, corporate comms) reflects genre norms more than personality. Weight uncontrollable features (function words, rhythm, hedging habits) and discount performed ones.
- **State vs. trait.** One angry email is a bad day, not a temperament. Only patterns stable across topics and time support trait claims.
- **Confounds**: non-native language proficiency, AI drafting assistance, heavy editing by others, medical or situational factors all masquerade as personality. Note any suspected confound in the limitations box.
- **Framework validity varies.** Big Five: strong science. DISC: reasonable for communication behavior. MBTI: popular but psychometrically weak — present as preference shorthand. Enneagram: motivational hypothesis only. The report labels each accordingly.

## Required report elements

Every delivered profile must contain:

1. A **limitations box** (sample size, genres, masking factors, confounds, what the analysis cannot claim).
2. **Confidence labels** on every trait claim (high / moderate / low), earned per the evidence rules in `frameworks.md`.
3. The sentence (or an equivalent): *"This is an informal analysis of writing style, not a psychological assessment, and may not reflect how this person is in other contexts."*

## Tone

Write profiles the subject could read without feeling attacked or flattered into misuse. Descriptive, specific, respectful; strengths and growth edges both grounded in quotes; no horoscope-style filler that fits everyone (Barnum statements — if a sentence would be true of most people, cut it).
